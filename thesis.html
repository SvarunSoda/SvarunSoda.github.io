PHOSCO

Phase-Based Oscillatory Computation

Abstract

The proposed Phase-based Oscillatory Computation (PHOSCO) framework provides an initial model for an oscillator-based general-purpose temporal computing system, operating in the continuous value-space. Analog numerical values were encoded as differences in relative oscillation phases between individual concurrently-running analog oscillator pairings, and represented as Time-Difference Variables (TDVs). Oscillator pausing mechanics were used to modify values stored as TDVs within the experimental model, and linear arithmetic operations for copying, inverting, adding and subtracting were made possible by the usage of conditional oscillator pausing actions. A preliminary validation of the proposed PHOSCO framework was carried out via the construction and evaluation of a basic prototype device. The constructed prototype used 6 custom-designed Controllable Oscillator (CO) modules, centered around the classic 555 capacitor-based oscillator, that included supplemental oscillation pausing & frequency-locking mechanics. The CO modules were designed as custom electrical circuits, and implemented via custom printed circuit boards, alongside the TLC555 oscillator module. Analog value storage and the proposed arithmetic operations were subsequently simulated and evaluated within the prototype device using high-speed digital controller systems. Storage longevity and operational precision were comprehensively evaluated by duty-cycle & relative phase difference tracking algorithms, with the resultant data being qualitatively analyzed using graphical visualizations. These evaluations included a discussion of inter-oscillatory dynamics and coupling forces. The implemented frequency-locking mechanics resulted in an operating duty-cycle convergence within ±0.83 μs of all CO modules. The simulated linear arithmetic operations displayed a high degree of precision, producing mathematically correct results within an expected tolerance of ±10% of the operating duty-cycle. The longevity of stored numerical values displayed a moderate amount of drift within a tracking period of 10 seconds. The attained results delivered a promising preliminary validation of the proposed PHOSCO framework, and offered exciting possibilities for further investigative efforts into unconventional temporal models. Future research avenues were suggested and discussed in terms of hypothetical evolutions and developments of the PHOSCO model, including explorations of electrical isolation improvements, noise characterization, power profile analysis and potential implementations of non-linear arithmetic operations.

Introduction

Research and development of generalized computational models forms the backbone of contemporary computer science research. The promise of a computing framework applicable to a wide variety of computational tasks has sparked countless hours of research, invested in by virtually all tenets of theoretical, experimental, statistical and artistic sciences since the early 20th century (see Chapter 2). However, following the initial match-up between the inaugural discrete-time digital frameworks and their continuous-time analog counterparts in the 1940s and 50s, the orderly, elegant and straightforward operational nature of digital systems has allowed them to surpass virtually all alternatives in the context of wider accessibility and raw computing capabilities. While the vast majority of the computational requirements present in most fields of modern engineering have been almost entirely satisfied with capable and efficient digital systems, an interest in the exploration of unconventional frameworks has remained in various theoretical and practical realms of study. Alongside allowing researchers to indulge in the highly intriguing, captivating and thought-provoking process of investigating unconventional computing and number-processing paradigms, this type of research has occasionally resurfaced throughout history in the scientific community to solve a variety of complex problems. In recent decades, a large portion of these investigative efforts have focused on developing computational models that are capable of outperforming their conventional counterparts in terms of power efficiency. The latest developments in generative artificial intelligence and deep-learning algorithms have revealed an increasingly hungry power demand profile of the existing computing infrastructure. Various unconventional digital and analog computational frameworks have thus been developed or resurfaced as part of the crucial task of reducing humanity's heavy energy footprint (see Section 2.1.2). In conjunction with this research effort, a considerable amount of emphasis has been placed on investigating alternate physical mediums for numerical encoding schemes. One of the most notable and interesting physical mediums analyzed within this research effort is time (see Section 2.2). To some degree, time-based logic is present in a majority of contemporary digital systems that are used extensively on an every-day basis. However, computational frameworks that rely entirely on time-based value-encoding schemes, operating fundamentally in the continuous value-space, have recently been proposed as potential alternatives for a variety of specialized and generalized computational tasks. Such paradigms, commonly referred to as Temporal Computing models, are likely to see a gradual increase in research focus across a variety of potential fields of application in the coming decade. To provide an insight into the viability, technical capabilities and limitations of temporal systems in the context of general-purpose numerical computation, a simple phase-based oscillatory temporal framework is investigated (see Chapter 3), implemented (see chapters 4 & 5) and evaluated (see Chapter 6) in this work.

The core essence of analog computation pertains to its ability to operate in domains of theoretically infinite precision. Such computing systems derive their etymology from the term "analogy", as they are often designed to systematically model the natural world [1]. Since our environment is largely analog in nature, these frameworks were researched and implemented extensively throughout history to perform a diverse range of computational tasks. Contemporary surveys often categorize most computing systems based on the time-domain and value-space in which they can function [2].

2.1.1  Continuous-time Analog Computation

Continuous-time analog computation is the traditional analog computing paradigm. The key characteristic of this model family lies in its ability to operate in the continuous value-space and the continuous time-domain, where computational tasks are carried out simultaneously in a real-time environment with fully analog values and variables [2][1]. This serves as a valuable tool for tasks involving actively-changing inputs and variables, such as solving complex differential equations [3][4][5][6] or carrying out advanced physics simulations [5][7][8]. A significant drawback of continuous-time analog systems is their precision [1][9][10]. Voltage or current are often used to represent fully analog values, and sensitive circuitry is commonly used to perform calculations. Therefore, the precision of these systems is highly dependent on the quality and reliability of their internal electrical components. This has made continuous-time analog computing systems historically difficult to manufacture and mass-produce [1][9]. Most early analog computing models developed in the 1930s and 40s belong to this category, as do the vast majority of all analog computers.

One of the earliest developed analog computers was Vannevar Bush's Differential Analyzer [11], made entirely from mechanical parts, and used for solving second-order differential equations. Similarly, one of the earliest general-purpose analog computer models was conceived by Claude Shannon, as the General-Purpose Analog Computer [12], which in many ways continues to serve as the basis for most continuous-time analog systems today.

2.1.2  Discrete-time Analog Computation

Discrete-time analog computation is the more obscure analog computing paradigm that has historically seen less development than continuous-time frameworks. The defining characteristic of this model family is the fact that computation in the continuous value-space is carried out in discretized steps [1][2]. This hybrid approach permits the existence of individual operation classes, similar to the operating principle of most digital systems, while maintaining the presence of fully analog values and variables [1]. Such systems are well-suited for non-deterministic computational tasks, including arithmetic operation approximation [16][17][18], audio processing [19][20] and neural network simulation [21][22][23][24]. Some surveys draw a distinction between discrete-time and sequential-time analog computing systems, where a further categorization is made on the presence of a consistent instruction execution frequency [1]. The lack of a strict simultaneous execution scheme, which is otherwise characteristic of their continuous-time counterparts (see Section 2.1.1), suggests that discrete-time analog models introduce an expansion of possible analog value storage & computational methodologies. This potential makes these frameworks exciting candidates for novel general-purpose computing model research.

2.2  Temporal Computation

Any computing framework requires some physical medium to encode the values and variables contained within its computational model. Historically this requirement was often satisfied through voltage [12][29][15] or current [30][31]. Another tangible physical medium that could be used to satisfy this requirement is time. Computing frameworks that operate with time-based value encoding schemes are commonly referred to as Temporal Computing models [32][33][34][35].

There are several key advantages that temporal computing systems maintain over their conventional digital & analog counterparts. One of the primary challenges encountered in modern computational research is the exponential rise in power demand of the existing computing infrastructure. With the recent emergence of generative artificial intelligence, computational power requirements are currently expected to double every three years [34]. Thus, low-power models are urgently sought after in order to reduce the heavy energy footprint of contemporary systems. To this end, temporal frameworks have recently been proposed as low-power alternatives to conventional methodologies [32][36]. Since information is not encoded through individual bits, these systems can theoretically encode large information packets on far fewer physical lines compared to their digital equivalents. This also suggests that temporal systems can hypothetically process the same information density as their digital counterparts with fewer electronic components. Experimental results have previously shown that temporal systems have significantly outperformed digital ones in terms of the minimization of physical footprints [34]. Moreover, while precision and accuracy sacrifices are expected when implementing temporal systems in most practical applications, certain algorithmic and graph-based processing tasks have previously displayed significant computational speed & efficiency advantages when executed with temporal architectures. For example, an experimental implementation of Djikstra's algorithm on a temporal state machine has shown significant promise in terms of energy and throughput [37]. It has also been demonstrated that various dynamic programming algorithms are particularly well-suited to temporal systems [38].

Despite the advantages that temporal computing models maintain over their conventional digital & analog equivalents, several key differences are important to highlight. Reading a value stored in a temporal system requires its respective storage mechanism to display its value to an observing logical entity or user at least once, which inherently requires a passage of time. Therefore any computation taking place within temporal models, by definition, cannot be instantaneous [32][36]. This fact suggests that discrete-time analog frameworks (see Section 2.1.2) are particularly well-suited for implementations of temporal systems, since their overall accuracy should not depend on a simultaneous execution scheme that is characteristic of their continuous-time counterparts (see Section 2.1.1).

2.2.1  Time-Difference Variables (TDVs)

Encoding a numerical value as an arbitrary passage of time is often referred to as a Time-Difference Variable (TDV) [33]. This abstract data structure can be implemented in various ways. For example, a TDV can be encoded as the elapsed time between the rising edges of two digital signals [33][34].

A notable contemporary example of temporal computing systems that use TDVs are hybrid hardware accelerators (see Section 3.4.2). These devices serve to accelerate existing digital systems and usually interface with them via time-to-digital converters [33], which explicitly convert a timed delay to a digital value. Another example of such systems are Time-Difference Circuits (see Section 3.4.4). These devices include time-domain circuitry that operate directly with numerical values encoded as time differences or timed delays. Analog timed latches can store a time delay as some analog medium, and are able to reproduce their stored values as a delay signal. Arithmetic operations are usually performed by introducing timed delays of the involved signals to achieve a relative phase change.

2.2.2  Phase-based Logic

Time-Difference Variables can also be implemented cyclically, where their time difference signals are displayed repeatedly, often at a defined display frequency. The cyclical & repeating nature of these types of TDVs also means their numerical values are inherently limited in representation range by their display frequency, often requiring them to be expressed as a relative phase difference (∆Φ) between cyclical digital signals, such as oscillators.

Similar examples of temporal computing systems that use cyclical TDVs are oscillator-based computing systems (see Section 3.4.1). Such frameworks use networks of concurrently running electrical oscillators and phase-based logic to perform computation. They usually rely on physical dynamics, such as oscillator coupling, to directly model the problems they are solving (see Section 3.4.1.1). Individual oscillator modules can also be used to simulate many digital circuits, using locked phase relationships to directly encode digital data (see Section 3.4.1.2). Another similar example of these systems is pulse-train signal processing (see Section 3.4.3). In this data processing framework, function waveforms are represented as a series of repeated electrical signal pulses, and arithmetic is performed by combining, adding or removing individual pulses in an algorithmic sequence.

Phase-based Oscillatory Computation (PHOSCO)

3.1  Framework Overview

The combined usage of cyclical TDVs and phase-based logic has seen relatively limited exploration in contemporary computational research. Their capabilities and technical limits, in the context of a theoretical general-purpose discrete-time analog computing system, will be explored in the proposed Phase-based Oscillatory Computation (PHOSCO) framework.

The PHOSCO model encodes an analog numerical value as the relative phase difference (∆Φ) between a pair of concurrently-running linear oscillators. Thus, the basic storage unit of a hypothetical PHOSCO-based system is a pairing of oscillator elements (see Section 3.2). Arithmetic operations are encoded in the relative phase relationships between multiple oscillator pairings. It relies on oscillator pausing mechanics (see Section 3.2.1) to achieve changes in ∆Φ oscillator pairing and perform numerical computation (see Section 3.3). This phase-based encoding scheme permits the proposed PHOSCO framework to effectively store analog numerical values within its computational model and perform basic arithmetic operations with them.

3.2  TDV Storage

In the proposed PHOSCO model, a volatile TDVs is implemented as a pair of concurrently-running oscillator elements, a "Value" oscillator element and a "Reference" oscillator element (labeled as VAL & REF respectively in subsequent diagrams). These oscillator elements maintain the same operating frequency and duty-cycle duration (tduty), but maintain an independent ∆Φ between their relative oscillation phases. In the proposed PHOSCO framework, the process of storing an analog numerical value into a TDV involves matching its ∆Φ, measured as the elapsed time from the rising edge of REF to the rising edge of VAL, to a proportional duration of time. All stored numerical values are expressed as scalars, defined within a range enclosed by ∆Φ = 0° (in-phase) and ∆Φ = 180° (out-of-phase). A larger ∆Φ between a given TDV's oscillator pairing elements indicates a larger stored numerical value.

In the proposed PHOSCO framework, the oscillator elements comprising all TDVs operate with analog oscillation states. Thus, each oscillator element is abstractly represented as repeated charging & discharging cycles, with its digital output state toggled upon its oscillation state reaching a maximum or minimum threshold. Each oscillator element can be arbitrarily started, interrupted and stopped in the continuous time-domain, effectively providing an analog value-space.

3.2.1  Oscillator Pausing

In the proposed PHOSCO framework, modifying the numerical value stored in a TDV is performed by changing its ∆Φ. This is achieved with temporary interruptions of the oscillations of the TDV's oscillator element(s), and is referred to as "pausing".

Oscillator Pausing

Interruption of an oscillator's oscillation cycle, while preserving its analog oscillation state.

Pausing one oscillator element of a given TDV for a duration of time effectively results in its ∆Φ being shifted, and its stored numerical value being changed (see Figure 3.3). Thus, in the context of the proposed PHOSCO framework, the terms "pause" and "∆Φ shift" are analogous.

In subsequent abstract TDV visualizations (see Figure 3.4), their VAL & REF oscillator element outputs are represented as OUT-VAL & OUT-REF, and their pausing mechanics are controlled by PAUSE-VAL & PAUSE-REF.

3.3  TDV Operations

In the proposed PHOSCO framework, arithmetic operations involving TDVs are performed by matching the ∆Φ of a chosen target TDV, referred to as the "Result TDV", to some linear combination of one or more chosen input TDVs, referred to as the "Operand TDVs". This is accomplished through the usage of the oscillator pausing mechanic (see Section 3.2.1) to match the relative oscillating phases the Result TDV's oscillator elements to some linear combination of the Operand TDVs' oscillator element signals. This phase-matching process of the Result TDV is referred to as the "Construction Step" of a TDV operation.

Construction Step

The Result TDV's ∆Φ is matched to some linear combination of the Operand TDVs' ∆Φ, as determined by the operation type.

Furthermore, to ensure the construction step of a given TDV operation matches the Result TDV's ∆Φ to the arithmetically correct value, operations involving multiple Operand TDVs require the relative alignment of their Operand TDVs' ∆Φ to reflect the appropriate linear combination. This relative alignment of the Operand TDVs is referred to as the "Alignment Step" of a TDV operation, and shifts one Operand TDV's ∆Φ, relative to the other Operand TDV's ∆Φ, to produce the correct alignment.

Alignment Step

The Operand TDVs' ∆Φ are aligned to reflect the appropriate linear combination of their values, as determined by the operation type.

These phase matching & alignment processes are performed by issuing conditional pausing actions on the Operand & Result TDVs' individual oscillator elements, as determined by a unique network of digital logic gates that continuously monitor their output states (see Figure 3.5). This digital logic gate network is referred to as the "Operation Circuit" (fop), with each TDV operation type containing a unique fop.

A TDV operation is started upon the connection of its fop to the Operand & Result TDVs oscillator element output signals, and is concluded upon the disconnection of its fop following an arbitrary duration of time. During this period, the Result TDV's ∆Φ value converges to the arithmetically correct result (see Figure 3.6), and is referred to as the "Operation Convergence Duration" (tocd). Thus, each TDV operation requires an inherent passage of time, corresponding to tocd, to assign the arithmetically correct ∆Φ to the Result TDV. An ideal minimum tocd is not explicitly established in the proposed PHOSCO framework.

The TDV Copy Operation accepts one Operand TDV and one Result TDV. The Result TDV's ∆Φ is assigned a value corresponding to the Operand TDV's ∆Φ (see Figure 3.8).

Brings the Result TDV's VAL & REF oscillator elements in-phase with the Operand TDV's VAL & REF oscillator elements respectively.

In the context of the proposed PHOSCO framework, this can be achieved by individually pausing the Result TDV's VAL & REF oscillator elements based on the outputs of two XOR logic gates respectively, the first one taking the two VAL oscillator elements of the Operand & Result TDVs as its inputs, and the second one taking the two REF oscillator elements of the Operand & Result TDVs as its inputs. The TDV Inversion Operation accepts one Operand TDV and one Result TDV. The Result TDV ∆Φ is assigned a value corresponding to the Operand TDV's ∆Φ, subtracted from 180° (see Figure 3.10).

TDV Inversion Operation - Construction Step

Brings the Result TDV's VAL & REF oscillator elements in-phase with the Result TDV's inverted REF & non-inverted VAL oscillator elements respectively.

In the context of the proposed PHOSCO framework, this can be achieved by individually pausing the Result TDV's VAL & REF oscillator elements based on the outputs of two XOR logic gates respectively, the first one taking the inverted REF oscillator element of the Operand TDV and the non-inverted VAL oscillator element of the Result TDV as its inputs, and the second one taking the REF oscillator element of the Result TDV and the VAL oscillator element of the Operand TDV as its inputs. 3.3.3  Addition

The TDV Addition Operation accepts two Operand TDVs and one Result TDV. The Result TDV's ∆Φ is assigned a value corresponding to a linear addition of the Operand TDVs' ∆Φ (see Figure 3.12).

Aligns the Operand TDVs' ∆Φ periods such that the 1st Operand TDV's ∆Φ falling edge matches the 2nd Operand TDV's ∆Φ rising edge.

In the context of the proposed PHOSCO framework, this can be achieved by simultaneously pausing the 1st Operand TDV's oscillator elements based on the output of an XOR logic gate taking the VAL oscillator element of the 1st Operand TDV and the REF oscillator element of the 2nd Operand TDV as its inputs.

TDV Addition Operation - Construction Step

Brings the Result TDV's VAL & REF oscillator elements in-phase with the VAL oscillator element of the 1st Operand TDV and the REF oscillator element of the 2nd Operand TDV respectively.

In the context of the proposed PHOSCO framework, this can be achieved by individually pausing the Result TDV's VAL & REF oscillator elements based on the outputs of two XOR logic gates respectively, the first one taking the VAL oscillator elements of the 2nd Operand & Result TDVs as its inputs, and the second one taking the REF oscillator elements of the 1st Operand & Result TDVs as its inputs. Thus, the TDV Addition operation's fop consists of three XOR digital logic gates (see Figure 3.11).

The TDV Subtraction Operation accepts two Operand TDVs and one Result TDV. The Result TDV ∆Φ assigned a value corresponding to a linear subtraction of the 1st Operand TDV from the 2nd Operand TDV (see Figure 3.14).

3.4  Related Works

In the rapidly evolving search for low-power alternatives to conventional computing frameworks, many temporal and oscillator-based models have been developed and implemented to perform a wide range of computational tasks.

3.4.1  Oscillator Networks

Similar oscillator-based temporal frameworks rely on dynamical relationships between the individual oscillators, such as coupling (see Section 4.4.1), to perform computation. This contrasts with the proposed PHOSCO model, in which there is a direct relationship between encoded analog values and relative phase differences between individual oscillators.

3.4.1.1  Coupled Oscillator Networks

The physical mechanics of harmonically coupled oscillators have been studied extensively [41][42][43][44]. Instead of working against the natural tendency of neighboring oscillators to be drawn towards each others' oscillating phases, many oscillator-based computing models work with this coupling force to achieve computation. Given nature's prevalence of synchronization patterns, most of these coupled oscillator frameworks are particularly suited for solving problems and making models of dynamical systems present in nature. A notable example of such implementations are Ising machines [45][46][47]. These devices are used to find solutions for NP-hard combinatorial problems, known as Ising optimization problems, which usually involve minimizing an overall energy state in a node-based system. The natural tendency of coupled oscillators to match each others' oscillating phase and frequency at the lowest possible energy level makes them ideal for finding steady-states in Ising problems. Another use case of coupled oscillator machines is modeling and training of neural networks systems and predictive models [48][49]. Large arrays of oscillatory networks exhibit natural synchronizations between individual oscillator pairs, which allows for dynamic changes and permits the overall system to exhibit learning patterns. This behavior makes these types of oscillator networks an ideal candidate for representing and modeling large scale neural network systems, specifically with predictive tasks such as musical melody prediction [48] and signal anomaly detection [49], with significant advantages over their digital counterparts in terms of power efficiency. Coupled oscillator network systems share similarities with the proposed PHOSCO framework in the shared usage of oscillators to perform computation. However, coupled oscillators systems rely entirely on the group dynamics encoded in coupling forces within their networks to attain solutions for the tasks they're solving. This contrasts with the individual phase-based interactions between oscillators used in the proposed PHOSCO framework, which relies on independent and pre-determined relative phase difference management in order to store analog values and perform computation.

3.4.1.2  Phase-based Digital Simulation

Similarly to the operating principle of coupled oscillator networks, individual oscillatory elements can also be selectively subjected to coupling forces to effectively draw their oscillating phases towards a fixed value. Such systems are well suited to simulate or represent a variety of digital systems. One of the earliest examples of a phase-based system that used coupled oscillators for digital data storage was Von Neumann's set of patents describing their proposed phase-based devices, such as the Phase-encoded Latch [50][51]. More recently, generalized models for digital information storage and simulation using coupled oscillators were developed [52][53]. Relative differences in oscillating phases between intentionally coupled oscillators were used to encode individual bits. Such phase-based devices have also been shown to demonstrate a significant degree of noise immunity, and fully-operational latching mechanisms were proposed as alternatives to conventional digital latching circuits [53]. These systems also rely on dynamical coupling forces to perform computation, albeit they do not use the generalized behavior of the network to calculate solutions for the tasks they solve, but are instead individually coupled to fixed reference values to represent binary systems. While this approach bears more similarities to the proposed PHOSCO framework in the usage of relative phase differences to encode information, they are most often used for representing and encoding digital values instead of analog ones.

3.4.2  Hybrid Hardware Accelerators

The topic of hybrid hardware accelerators has seen increasing focus in recent years. With the increasing computational weight being exerted on the existing digital computing infrastructure, many complementary temporal models have been developed aimed at improving their operation in terms of speed and power efficiency. One example of such a previously proposed temporal device [34] uses temporal bistream data structures to represent digital values, where a pre-determined elapse of time within a digital signal sequentially encodes a set of bits in a digital number. Another notable example of such devices includes a vector-by-matrix multiplier hardware accelerator [54], in which analog vector-by-matrix multiplication was carried out by switch-capacitor-based multiplier circuits. Current-mode devices were used to manipulate and precisely control charging & discharging of capacitor-based delay-line modules, which allowed numerical information to be encoded as time differences and arithmetic operations to be performed. Memristor-based temporal systems were also previously modeled in the context of graph computations [37]. A temporal memory cell was constructed from a series of delay-line elements, each consisting of a set of memristor circuits. Each memristor effectively acted as a resistor that changes it's resistance based on historical current flows, which was used to encode streams of digital bits from a measured time delay. This memristor-based temporal memory cell was capable of storing and displaying its data, relying on a read signal to be delivered to commence its value displaying process. Temporal hardware accelerator devices share many similarities with the proposed PHOSCO framework, notably in the discrete-time execution nature and time difference value encoding. A hypothetical PHOSCO-based system could likewise be used as an accelerator of existing digital systems in a similar operating principle to many of the previously proposed temporal hardware accelerators.

3.4.3  Pulse-Train Processing

A similar form of time-domain data & signal processing has previously been modeled as pulse-train processing [55]. Such frameworks propose a generalized model for time-domain arithmetic operations of addition, multiplication and convolution, in mathematical functions encoded as a series of electrical pulses. Rigorous mathematical proofs were proposed and analyzed, in which the modalities of linear [56] and non-linear [57] mathematical operations are studied in the context of temporal logic. This form of analog signal processing carries similarities to the proposed PHOSCO framework in terms of the continuous value-space computation performed with analog values encoded as cyclical time-differences. However, the PHOSCO model was proposed as a more fundamental framework designed to operate with individual numerical values instead of functional waveforms.

3.4.4  Time-Difference Circuits

A series of time-difference circuits were previously proposed as a series of time-domain digital logical modules that performed analog TDV storage encoded as pairs of digital signals [39]. Similar TDV operation modules were also previously proposed, including time-domain adder and subtractor modules that performed arithmetic operations by artificially delaying the involved digital signals to achieve a relative phase difference change. An integer time-domain multiplier module was also presented, the Time-difference Amplifier [39], which effectively multiplied a TDV by an arbitrary integer value. A series of time difference analog latch circuits were also previously proposed [58][40]. These circuits also operated with analog values encoded as timing differences between digital signals, and stored them by intermittently discharging a holding capacitor. They would display the output value by discharging the remaining charge upon the receipt of a query signal, which allows for a single display process of the previously saved TDV. Such devices share similarities with the proposed PHOSCO framework in the exact encoding of analog values as time differences between digital signal edges, as well as performing linear arithmetic operations of TDV addition and subtraction. Most of the proposed circuits could be used as building blocks of a hypothetical PHOSCO-based system, especially in transient configurations that allow for repeated signal displays to support encoding of cyclical TDVs. Albeit, additional logic would have to be introduced to ensure TDV signal consistency, such as frequency and duty-cycle locking mechanisms, and allow for the continuous-time pausing actions that are characteristic of the proposed PHOSCO framework.

4.2  Controllable Oscillator (CO) Design

The basic building block of the constructed PHOSCO prototype is a custom-designed Controllable Oscillator (CO) electrical circuit, centered around the conventional "555" capacitor-based oscillator.

Capacitor-based Oscillator

Circuit which generates a digital clock signal via repeated charging & discharging cycles of a timing capacitor [59][60][61].

The analog oscillating medium used by a capacitor-based oscillator, such as the 555 timer, is the charge stored in a timing capacitor attached to the oscillator's internal circuitry. The oscillator module toggles its output digital state upon the timing capacitor's stored charge reaching pre-determined thresholds during its oscillating cycles. The capacitance of the timing capacitor affects the output frequency. Common use cases for capacitor-based oscillators include low-power clock generation [62], function signal generators [63] and environmental sensing [64].

The custom CO modules implemented in the PHOSCO prototype are centered around the TLC555 CMOS-based integrated oscillator circuit [60]. It shares similarities with conventional 555 astable timer circuits [59] (see Figure 4.1), with the inclusion of additional logic to support the continuous-time oscillator pausing mechanics used in the proposed PHOSCO framework.

A pair of analog switches and an SR-latch are used to effectively isolate the timing capacitor, normally attached to the TRIGGER, DISCHARGE & THRESHOLD connections of a conventional 555 timer module through a pair of diodes (see Figure 4.2). This setup provides the capability of effectively pausing the oscillating cycles of the CO modules while preserving their analog oscillation states. The supplemental pausing logic is controlled by a digital input signal, denoted as the PAUSE input connection node, where the pausing mechanism is engaged when the PAUSE node is brought HIGH. Additionally, the ENABLE input node and the SIGNAL output node are connected to the RESET and OUT connections of the TLC555 module respectively. While conventional 555 timer modules are not designed for high-precision applications, with an estimated initial oscillation precision of ±1%, a 50 ppm/C° timing-temperature drift [59] and a non-linear oscillation curve, a key modification is introduced into these custom CO modules aimed at improving their frequency & tduty matching capabilities. The charging & discharging resistors that control the HIGH & LOW tduty of their generated output signals, referred to as "timing resistors" and normally attached to the TRIGGER, DISCHARGE and THRESHOLD connections of a conventional 555 timer module, are replaced with custom-designed controllable variable resistors, referred to as "Frequency Control Resistors" (FCRs). The design of these FCR elements consisted of a controllable digital potentiometer with a high degree of resistance resolution (see Section 4.2.1), to allow precise control of tduty pertaining to the output signals generated by the CO modules. A pair of FCR elements was present in each CO module (see Figure 4.2), and were controlled by the Frequency-Locking Logic implemented on the FLL MCU (see Section 4.3.2). The FCR element whose connection nodes were connected to the primary voltage source & the 555 modules's DISCHARGE connection, referred to as "FCR A", was responsible for controlling the HIGH tduty duration of the output signal. The FCR element whose connection nodes were connected to the 555 modules's DISCHARGE & TRIGGER connections, referred to as "FCR B", was responsible for controlling the LOW tduty duration of the output signal. Increasing the effective resistance of an FCR element increases the duration of the tduty duration its responsible for. Therefore, by using a 1 μF timing capacitor, the custom CO modules were expected to operate at an operating frequency 250 Hz, corresponding to a tduty duration of 2,000 μs.

Each CO module was subsequently connected to the Frequency-Locking & Operation MCUs via a set of digital data lines pertaining to each MCU. The connection set pertaining to the Operation MCU consisted of 3 data lines (see Table 4.2), which allowed for effective control of the relative oscillation phases of each CO module and by the TDV Simulation Logic (see Section 4.3.3). The connection set pertaining to the Frequency-Locking MCU consisted of 5 data lines (see Table 4.3), which allowed for effective control of the individual CO module's operating frequency & tduty by the Frequency-Locking Logic (see Section 4.3.2).

4.2.1  Frequency-Control Resistor (FCR) Design

The design of the Frequency-Control Resistor (FCR) elements implemented in each CO module consisted of a digital potentiometer circuit, with a resolution of 1 Ω per resistance step, and a precision of 12 bits (see Figure 4.3). This gave an estimated possible resistance range of 0 Ω to 4,096 Ω for each FCR element.

This resistor network was controlled by a set of 12 digital data lines. It included a set of 12 resistive sections, corresponding to effective resistances of 1 Ω, 2 Ω, 4 Ω, 8 Ω, 16 Ω, 32 Ω, 64 Ω, 128 Ω, 256 Ω, 512 Ω, 1,024 Ω & 2,048 Ω. Each resistive section was connected in series through a set of 12 SPDT analog switches, which were controlled by a 12-bit bi-directional digital counter. This 12-bit counter element was designed with a set of 12 JK Flip-Flop circuit elements, and connected to the FLL MCU via a set of 3 digital data lines (see Table 4.3).

This FCR design allowed for incremental resistive steps of approximately 1 Ω upon the arrival of a rising edge of the FCR's SET digital data line. Each resistive step direction was determined by the state of the DIR digital data line at the moment of increment, with a state of HIGH corresponding to an increase and a state of LOW corresponding to a decrease. This setup was expected to produce tduty duration steps of approximately 11 μs at each effective resistance increment.

4.3  Logic Design

The custom CO elements that comprised the constructed PHOSCO prototype were controlled & managed by a set of three logical modules (see Figure 4.4), developed & implemented on a pair of high-speed digital microcontroller systems (MCUs). Each MCU system consisted of a simple digital microprocessor, with the capabilities of managing a set of digital data lines, executing logical instruction sequences written as high-level code and measuring timing intervals on a reasonably accurate scale. These MCU systems controlled the individual CO elements via a set of digital data lines (see Tables 4.2 & 4.3), which permitted their logical elements to perform frequency-locking operations, TDV simulation and precision evaluations on the constructed prototype.

The frequency-locking logic module (see Section 4.3.2), responsible for matching the tduty durations of all CO modules in the constructed prototype, was implemented on the Frequency-Locking MCU (FLL MCU). The TDV simulation logic module (see Section 4.3.3) and the evaluation logic (see Section 4.3.4), responsible for storing analog numerical values as TDVs (see Section 3.2), simulating arithmetic TDV operations (see Section 4.3.3.2) and performing tduty & ∆Φ measurements, were implemented on the Operation MCU.

The use of high-speed digital MCUs to implement & simulate the various logical elements of the constructed PHOSCO prototype, instead of designing & implementing dedicated control & evaluation circuitry, was undertaken for three primary reasons.

Firstly, this approach was expected to enable a straightforward & modular development cycle of the various logical elements comprising the constructed PHOSCO prototype, while maintaining a relatively high degree of operational & evaluation accuracy. The usage of code-based logical algorithms for simulation & evaluation tasks, instead of designing dedicated circuit modules for each of the logical tasks, greatly reduced the overall complexity of the prototype design & construction process (see Chapter 5). This allowed for the development of a comprehensive set of evaluation procedures, and a straightforward evaluation data visualization workflow (see Section 4.3.4).

Secondly, this approach was expected to reduce the overall amount of electrical noise present in the system. Dedicated control circuit elements for the frequency-locking, TDV simulation & evaluation logical modules would likely rely on additional oscillators or timers to provide references for tduty matching processes & evaluation procedures (see Section 6.2.1). This approach would maintain a high likelihood of introducing further electrical disturbances into all implemented oscillator modules, in addition to the already present coupling interactions that are characteristic of concurrently-running electrical oscillators (see Section 4.4.1).

Thirdly, this approach was expected to greatly reduce the overall construction costs and lead times encountered during the design & construction process of the PHOSCO prototype (see Chapter 5). Since each implemented CO module was built with custom-designed analog & digital circuits (see Section 5.1.1), these custom circuit modules were fabricated & assembled as printed circuit boards (PCBs) by a contemporary circuit board manufacturer (see Section 5.1.3). The fabricated & assembled PCB elements were subsequently connected together via manual soldering, which required a further investment of time and resources (see Section 5.1.4). Including additional dedicated control circuit elements for each logical module would have significantly increased the construction costs and lengthened the construction process of the constructed PHOSCO prototype.

Despite the mentioned advantages brought by the usage of digital MCU systems for the development & execution of the logical elements comprising the constructed PHOSCO prototype, a significant expected drawback of this approach was the degree of error in the accuracy of the simulation & evaluation procedures executed on the constructed prototype. Since the proposed PHOSCO framework operates in the continuous value-space, the usage of digital MCU systems to perform timing measurements was expected to inherently introduce a degree of error in all evaluation datasets. Thus, it was crucial to choose a digital MCU component with a very high operating speed, while maintaining a relatively simple programming protocol & interface (see Section 4.3.1).

4.3.1  Teensy 4.1 Digital Microcontroller (MCU)

The digital MCU component chosen for the implementation of the frequency-locking, operation simulation & evaluation logic of the constructed PHOSCO prototype was the Teensy 4.1 microcontroller, produced by the company PJRC. The Teensy 4.1 contains a Cortex-M7 ARM-based processor, operating at a default CPU speed of 600 MHz, alongside 8 MB of FLASH memory and 1 MB of RAM [65]. It supports 42 digital input & output connections operating at 3.3V, which are easily accessible via standard-size physical pins, alongside a pre-installed micro-SD card slot. It supports a straightforward programming interface using the Arduino IDE software development platform, with program sequences written as high-level C++ code and programmed via UART-based protocols over a USB connection (see Section 5.1.5). These features represent a significant speed & memory improvement over contemporary Arduino-based microcontroller systems, commonly manufactured with CPU speeds of under 20 MHz, far lower FLASH memory size and lacking pre-installed micro-SD card support. The fast operating speed, extensive set of accessible digital input & output lines, micro-SD card support, a simple programming interface and relatively low cost made the Teensy 4.1 an ideal component choice for the implementation of the FLL & Operation MCUs.

Additionally, unlike most other Arduino-based microcontrollers, the Teensy 4.1 possesses the ability to measure timing intervals in the sub- μs range. This is accomplished with the usage of the ARM_DWT_CYCCNT internal register, present in most conventional ARM processors. Being incremented on every CPU instruction cycle, accessing that internal register permits timing measurements in the sub- μs range with a known CPU clock speed, after being properly initialized at MCU startup. This feature allows for a higher degree of accuracy of all timing measurements and evaluations performed on the Teensy 4.1.

4.3.2  Frequency-Locking Logic

The frequency-locking logic used in the constructed PHOSCO prototype, implemented on the Frequency-Locking MCU, relied on repeated comparisons of the HIGH and LOW tduty durations achieved by all CO modules to a reference target duration. Following a tduty duration comparison, the measured CO's respective FCR module is altered to more closely match the tduty of its output signal to the target duration. This FLL logic was implemented with a custom closed-loop algorithm.

During an iteration of the custom frequency-locking algorithm (see Algorithm 4.1), defined as the FLLRunIter function (see Listing L.1, lines 171–207), the HIGH and LOW tduty durations of a CO module's output signal module are repeatedly queried until a state change is detected. This polling-based tduty duration measurement was implemented in the OscillatorGetCycles function (see Listing L.1, lines 232–296), and measured in CPU cycles. If the measured tduty did not match the target duration, provided as the operatingCycles argument, the FILL-SET data line of the measured CO's respective FCR element was brought HIGH for a brief duration of time, starting at the conclusion of the tduty duration measurement. The rising edge of the FLL-SET data line changed the effective resistance of its respective FCR element by triggering its 12-bit counter on a rising edge, and incrementing it in the direction determined by its FLL-DIR data line. The respective FCR's FLL-DIR data line was simultaneously changed depending on the measured duration. If the measured tduty duration was greater than the target duration, the respective FLL-DIR data line was brought LOW, resulting in a decrease of the respective FCR element's effective resistance, and a decrease in the CO's respective tduty duration (see figures 4.6 & 4.7) (see Listing L.1, lines 339–364). If the measured tduty duration was smaller than the target duration, the respective FLL-DIR data line was brought HIGH, resulting in an increase the respective FCR element's effective resistance, and an increase in the CO's respective tduty duration (see figures 4.6 & 4.7) (see Listing L.1, lines 311–336).

This tduty comparison and adjustment action was sequentially repeated for all CO modules connected to the FLL MCU, and executed iteratively in the FLLRun function (see Listing L.1, lines 110–168). The custom frequency-locking algorithm was executed for an indefinite duration of time on the FLL MCU, and its performance was expected to depend greatly on the operating frequencies of the CO modules and the CPU speed of the FLL MCU. Since the algorithm performs repeated measurements of each CO module's output by waiting for rising & falling edges in their output signal, it was expected to be most effective when the operating CPU speed of the FLL MCU was held much higher than the operating frequencies of the CO modules. The frequency-locking logic operated independently of any simulation & evaluation processes undertaken by the TDV simulation & evaluation logic.

4.3.3  TDV Simulation Logic

The TDV storage and operation simulation logic used in the constructed PHOSCO prototype, implemented on the Operation MCU, was responsible for controlling the individual CO modules, storing values in the 3 simulated TDVs, reading their values and performing arithmetic operations with them. It relied on the oscillator pausing mechanics (see Section 3.2.1), implemented in each CO module (see Section 4.2), to issue periodic pausing actions on them in order to achieve the desired ∆Φ changes between CO pairings and simulate the ∆Φ alignment of TDV operations.

The TDV storage simulation logic (see Section 4.3.3.1) used a custom algorithm that matched a simulated TDV's ∆Φ to an input numerical value, provided within a range between 0.0 and 1.0. It used another custom algorithm to read the ∆Φ of a simulated TDV, likewise returning the measured duration as an output numerical value between 0.0 and 1.0. The TDV operation simulation logic (see Section 4.3.3.2) used a set of custom algorithms that performed the ∆Φ matching & alignment actions pertaining to each TDV operation type (see Section 3.3). All simulated TDV operations could be executed arbitrarily with any simulated TDV in the constructed PHOSCO prototype.

4.3.3.1  TDV Storage Simulation

The TDV storage simulation logic used a custom algorithm that proportionally matched the ∆Φ between a simulated TDV's CO pairing to an input value. This algorithm relied on the usage of the oscillator pausing mechanic to issue pausing actions on the target TDV's VAL oscillator element, until the TDV's ∆Φ matched the desired value.

This algorithm (see Algorithm 4.2), implemented in the TDVSet function (see Listing L.2, lines 1239–1261), accepts the target TDV as the tdv argument, and the input numerical value as the value argument. The input value was provided as a decimal number ranging between 0.0 and 1.0, corresponding to a ∆Φ range between 0° (in-phase) and 180° (out-of-phase). The algorithm subsequently converts the provided input value to a corresponding time duration, measured in CPU cycles, and executes the TDVSetCycles function (see Listing L.2, lines 1265–1326), which proceeds to wait until a rising edge of the TDV's REF oscillator element was encountered. During this first waiting period, the target TDV's VAL oscillator element is paused if status is HIGH. The algorithm subsequently waits for a duration of time corresponding to the input value, previously calculated in the TDVSet function, during which the target TDV's VAL oscillator element is again paused if its state is HIGH. Finally, the algorithm subsequently waits for a duration of tduty, during which the target TDV's VAL oscillator element is paused if its output state is LOW. Following this waiting & pausing sequence executed in the TDVSetCycles function, the changed ∆Φ value of the target TDV was verified by executing the TDV ∆Φ reading algorithm (see Algorithm 4.3), and comparing measured value to the desired input. If the read ∆Φ value closely matched the desired input, as determined by the tolerance argument, the TDV storage algorithm is concluded.

The custom algorithm for reading the ∆Φ value of a simulated target TDV relied on a precise measurement of the elapsed time duration between the rising edges of the target TDV's REF & VAL oscillator elements.

This algorithm (see Algorithm 4.3), implemented in the TDVGet function (see Listing L.2, lines 1331–1345), accepts the target TDV as the tdv argument. The algorithm subsequently proceeds to execute the TDVGetCycles function (see Listing L.2, lines 1349–1380), which waits until a rising edge of the target TDV's REF oscillator element was encountered. The algorithm subsequently stored the current CPU cycle iteration by reading the value of the ARM_DWT_CYCCNT internal register, and proceeds to wait until a rising edge in the target TDV's VAL oscillator element was encountered. The elapsed time was then calculated by subtracting the current CPU cycle iteration from the previously-stored one, and the calculated ∆Φ was returned to the TDVGet function as a time duration, measured in CPU cycles. Finally, the algorithm converts the measured ∆Φ to a value between 0.0 & 1.0, and the algorithm is concluded by the return of this final decimal value.

These two algorithms, implemented in the TDVSet & TDVGet functions respectively, were arbitrarily executable by other elements of the TDV simulation & evaluation logic modules.

These custom algorithms directly simulated the digital logic gates comprising the fop of each operation type, and issued pausing actions on the provided Operand & Result TDVs' oscillator elements as determined by their respective fop circuits.

Finally, the algorithm ensures that the ∆Φ of the Result TDV is located within the valid range of 0° & 180°, and concludes the simulation of the Copy operation.

This algorithm (see Algorithm 4.5), implemented in the TDVInvert function (see Listing L.2, lines 1117–1137), accepts the Operand TDV as the operandTdv argument, and the Result TDV as the resultTdv argument.   • Frequency-Locking Precision
    • evaluate effectiveness of CO modules' frequency & tduty matching abilities
  • Storage Precision
    • evaluate effectiveness & accuracy of simulated TDVs
    • measure longevity of stored TDV values
  • Operation Precision
    • explore ideal tocd for each TDV operation type
    • evaluate effectiveness & accuracy of simulated TDV operations

4.3.4.3  Operation Precision

In order to evaluate the precision & result longevity of the TDV operations simulated in the constructed PHOSCO prototype, a set of testing algorithms aimed at tracking the resultant ∆Φ values produced by each operation type were developed as part of the evaluation logic module. These algorithms relied on repeated & consistent measurements of the ∆Φ durations pertaining to the TDVs involved in each operation test run, immediately after the execution of a given operation's simulation algorithm (see Section 4.3.3.2), over a predetermined set of tocd values.

This algorithm (see Algorithm 4.10), implemented for each operation type in the EvaluateTDVCopy (see Listing L.2, lines 744–883), EvaluateTDVInversion (see Listing L.2, lines 600–740), EvaluateTDVAddition (see Listing L.2, lines 395–596) and EvaluateTDVSubtraction (see Listing L.2, lines 190–391) functions respectively, executed the TDVSet algorithm for the Operand TDVs, over a pre-determined set of testing values (see Appendix B), defined as testValues. Each execution of the TDVSet algorithm on the Operand TDVs was followed by a ∆Φ tracking session of the Result TDV for a specified number of iterations, provided as the iters argument. Each ∆Φ measurement represented the measured elapsed time duration between the rising edge of the Result TDV's REF oscillator element, and the rising edge of its VAL oscillator element, measured in CPU cycles. These ∆Φ measurements were stored in a large pre-allocated array, defined as cycleDiffBuff, alongside a timestamp value for reach reading. The measured durations were stored in one TXT file per test value on the micro-SD card connected to the Operation MCU, with the same data formatting used in the storage evaluation logic. This set of testing algorithms was used for two types of evaluations pertaining to the simulated TDV operations.

The first evaluation metric that was investigated using this testing algorithm was determining whether each TDV operation type maintained a unique set of ideal tocd values. To this end, the testing algorithm of each TDV operation was executed with a wide range of tocd values, as defined by the tocdValues argument, in order to effectively conduct a parameter-space search for tocd in each TDV operation type. This parameter-space exploration task was conducted with very brief ∆Φ tracking sessions, to focus the attained results solely on the initial resultant values immediately after the execution of an operation. Using the same data storage scheme described in the TDV Storage evaluation section (see Section 4.3.4.2), the attained ∆Φ data contained a set of results pertaining to each tested tocd value. This data was subsequently parsed & processed with a custom Python-based visualization script (see Listing M.2, lines 402–463), which collected the result error average & standard deviation figures of all test conducted for each operation type, and correlated them to the respective tocd values they were tested with. This resulted in a set of graphical plots that visualized the average Result TDV error & deviation, with respect to the tested tocd values (see Section 5.2.3). The ideal tocd values for each TDV operation type could then be visually assessed for each tested input by observing any areas in the resultant plots that displayed a very low degree of average error & deviation.

Following the tocd parameter-space search, assuming an ideal set of values for tocd was found for all operations, each TDV operation type was further evaluated with an expanded set of test inputs and their respective ideal tocd values. The same testing algorithm was used, with the ideal tocd values as a singular parameter, and with longer ∆Φ tracking sessions executed immediately after each operation execution. This data was subsequently parsed & processed with a custom Python-based visualization script (see Listing M.2, lines 224–400), which collected the result error average & standard deviation figures of all tests conducted, and visualized the average Result TDV error & deviation for each operation type. These visualizations contained graphical plots showing the average Result TDV error & deviation, with respect to time, for each operation type (see Section 5.2.3). The overall measured ∆Φ error of the results produced by each operation, with their ideal tocd values, could then be visually assessed as a function of time for each operation type.

Furthermore, in order to discover any potential correlations between the observed result error for each operation type and the input values included in each testing set, a further set of graphical visualizations was created to assess these possibilities. These visualizations contained graphical plots showing the average Result TDV error & deviation of each operation type, correlated to their respective test input values supplied as the Operand TDVs during the testing sequences (see Listing M.2, lines 465–545). The overall measured ∆Φ error of the results produced by each one of the tested inputs in each operation type could then be visually assessed, in terms of their similarity to an error of 0%.

4.3.4.4  Expected Results

In order to account for the expected degree of error included in all time duration & ∆Φ measurements due to digital MCU simulation & electrical noise (see Section 4.4), an overall error tolerance threshold of ±10%, of tduty, was imposed on all evaluation metrics. Each simulated TDVs' ∆Φ was tracked over a duration of 10 seconds, immediately after the execution of the storage simulation algorithm, over all the test inputs. At the conclusion of each 10 second tracking session, the final measured ∆Φ was expected to not exceed an experienced drift & divergence of ±10% from its expected value. The immediate results produced by each simulated TDV operation were likewise expected to not exceed an experienced drift & divergence of ±10% from the mathematically correct values. Thus, if the overall ∆Φ error, measured within 10 seconds of storage and immediately following a TDV operation, did not exceed a figure of ±10% of the target tduty used by all CO modules within the prototype system, the prototype was considered as an effective validation of the proposed PHOSCO framework.

Given the highly experimental design nature of the PHOSCO prototype (see Section 4.2) and the limited construction capabilities encountered during its implementation (see Section 4.4.3), it was expected to display considerably higher power usage relative to contemporary temporal systems (see Section 3.4). Since the primary goal of the constructed prototype was validating the theoretical PHOSCO framework, any evaluations of its generalized power demand profile were not expected to reflect any potential power efficiency advantages it might maintain over similar frameworks.

4.4  Expected Challenges

Given the precise analog nature of the constructed PHOSCO prototype, several key challenges were expected to be encountered during its construction and evaluation. Most notable of these challenges are oscillator coupling, evaluation accuracy error and construction costs.

4.4.1  Oscillator Coupling

The most significant challenge faced during the construction and evaluation of the constructed PHOSCO prototype was oscillator coupling.

Oscillator Coupling

General tendency of concurrently-running electrical oscillators, powered by the same power source, to actively pull their oscillating frequencies & relative phases together. The overall intensity of this phenomenon is proportional to the frequency similarity of the affected oscillators [42][68].

Small disturbances in the power supply shared by the affected oscillator modules, caused by their repeated state-switches and oscillations, are the primary cause for the presence of this phenomenon [42]. In the context of the proposed PHOSCO framework, this coupling force is a major challenge because all involved oscillators rely on independent relative phase management to store values and perform computation. Since all oscillators in the constructed PHOSCO prototype were powered by the same DC electrical source (see Section 5.1.4), several important measures were undertaken in order to mitigate this negative effect. Each CO module was partially isolated via the use of decoupling capacitors, placed in series with small resistors, in order to provide a localized transient power source during disturbance events caused by other oscillations (see Section 5.1.1). Additionally, MCP1700 voltage regulators [69] were placed on the power supply lines of each CO module to further stabilize their local power supplies.

While the phenomenon of oscillator coupling is strictly interpreted as a negative occurrence in the context of the proposed PHOSCO framework, it is important to note that many contemporary oscillator-based systems are designed to incorporate, or entirely rely on, this behavior to perform computation (see Section 3.4.1).

4.4.2  Evaluation Accuracy

Despite the simplistic design of the constructed PHOSCO prototype, there were many expected sources of noise disturbance and procedural degrees of error introduced by the nature of the implementation. The usage of digital MCU systems to partially simulate & evaluate the experimental system was expected to introduce a certain degree of error in all performed evaluations that involved any timing measurements. This degree of error was expected to be relatively minimal due to the operating speed of the chosen digital MCU components (see Section 4.3.1), the optimization of the developed high-level simulation code (see Section 5.1.5) and the relatively low operating frequencies of the implemented CO modules (see Section 4.2). Regardless, the presence of a non-negligible degree of error was expected in all evaluation datasets. Furthermore, a degree of operational error was further expected from the inherent delays experienced by all conventional electrical components & logical modules that were used to build the constructed prototype. While the exact magnitude of the degree of error introduced by these unavoidable sources was difficult to ascertain, it was expected to not exceed the overall tolerance figure of ±10% of tduty in all evaluation datasets (see Section 4.3.4.4).

4.4.3  Construction Costs

The customized nature of most of the circuits involved in the constructed PHOSCO prototype required the design & fabrication of custom, pre-assembled printed circuit boards (PCBs) (see Section 5.1.3). This, alongside the required purchasing of supplemental electrical equipment for assembly & evaluation (see Section 5.1.2), was expected to introduce significant construction costs during the design & construction process. Therefore, it was important to choose an adequate PCB fabrication & assembly service to minimize the overall fabrication & assembly costs. Additionally, at the time of construction of the PHOSCO prototype, global trade tariffs were in effect for most imports into the United States, which included printed circuit board components. This extraneous circumstance led to a further increase in construction costs and significantly lengthened the construction process due to acquisition delays.

4.5  Related Works

A family of electrical circuits that shares many similarities with the custom Controllable Oscillator design implemented in the constructed PHOSCO prototype are Frequency-Locked Loops (FLLs). These circuits produce a digital clock signal at a desired frequency, which is a commonly shared goal with the oscillator modules pertaining to the proposed PHOSCO framework. Common use cases for conventional FLL circuits include power grid frequency estimation [70][71], navigational tracking system calibration & noise isolation [72][73] and environment sensor interfaces [74][75]. The choice to design custom controllable oscillator modules, as opposed to using existing FLL solutions, was undertaken for three primary reasons; duty-cycle control requirements, design simplicity and digital MCU interfacing. Independent duty-cycle control is often not a standard feature of contemporary frequency-locking modules. While there are existing combinations of FLL and duty-cycle controller circuits [76][77][78], the vast majority of them either require individual transistor-level implementations or complex fabrication techniques. Moreover, most commonly available FLL modules do not possess a straightforward Arduino-compatible digital MCU controller interfacing scheme. Given these design requirements, and since the scope of the experimental PHOSCO implementations only covered conventional circuit board manufacturing and Arduino-based controller systems, it was deemed appropriate to explore custom oscillator designs instead of applying existing FLL variants.

The constructed PHOSCO prototype was built with 6 custom designed Controllable Oscillator (CO) modules (see Section 4.2). The circuits comprising the CO modules were initially developed & tested in the Falstad Circuit Simulator (see Section 5.1.1). Subsequently, the CO modules circuits were converted to conventional circuit schematics and printed circuit board (PCB) layout designs using the KiCad circuit designer software. These PCB layout designs were exported and submitted for fabrication & assembly by the JLCPCB company, and delivered to the final place of assembly (see Section 5.1.3). Each CO module was implemented as a set PCBs, assembled together via soldered connections, and connected to the Frequency-Locking MCU & the Operation MCU via a set of physical connections, implemented as soldered wires (see Section 5.1.4).

The prototype system, including the FLL & Operation MCUs, were powered by a single +5V DC power source (see Figure 5.1). The FLL & Operation MCUs were individually connected to a Windows system via USB connections for programming via UART-based serial connections (see Section 5.1.5), using the Arduino IDE software development platform. During each evaluation session, the evaluation data was saved on a micro-SD card connected to the Operation MCU. Upon the conclusion of each evaluation session, the micro-SD card was connected to a Windows system, where the data was transferred for analysis using a micro-SD card adapter.

5.1.1  Circuit Design & Simulation

Each custom CO module implemented in the constructed PHOSCO prototype was built from a set of circuit elements, including an oscillator element, a pair of Frequency-Control Resistor (FCR) elements (see Section 4.2.1), and a pair of intermediate circuits that acted as connectors between the oscillator element and its FCR elements. All CO modules were connected to the FLL & Operation MCUs via a further intermediate circuit. Thus, the overall prototype consisted of 5 unique circuit designs (see Figure 5.2).

All circuits were initially developed & tested in the Falstad Circuit Simulator, on version 4.0.1 [79], in order to ensure that each of the designed CO modules performed the required tduty matching & pausing requirements as expected. The circuit designs were subsequently converted into individual schematics in the KiCad circuit designer software, on version 8.0.6 [80].

The oscillator circuit element (see Figure 5.3) was constructed around a TLC555 integrated circuit, marked as OSC1. It included a pair of analog switches, marked as SW1, that could effectively isolate the timing capacitor, marked as C6, from the oscillator component. Breakout connectors were made for each digital control line connected to the Operation MCU (see Table 4.2), alongside breakout connectors for the analog lines connected to the oscillator's FCR elements (see Table 4.3), marked as CONN_DATA1. The TLC555 component, alongside the analog switches, were partially decoupled from the shared power supply via the usage of a pair of decoupling capacitors, marked as C1 - C4, each placed after a decoupling resistor, marked as R1 & R2.

The FCR circuit element (see Figure 5.4) was constructed with a physical resistor network with a 1 Ω precision, connected together via a set of 12 analog switches, marked as SW1 - SW4, that were controlled by a 12-bit bi-directional digital counter. This 12-bit counter was constructed from 12 JK Flip-Flop components, marked as JK1 - JK6, which acted as a bi-directional digital counter with a reset toggle. Breakout connectors were made for each digital control line connected to the FLL MCU, alongside breakout connectors for the analog lines connected to the oscillator circuit element, marked as CONN_DATA1. The analog switches were partially decoupled with a pair of decoupling capacitors per each component, marked as C1 - C8, each placed after a decoupling resistor, marked as R91 - R94.

The two FCR circuit elements, that were assigned to each oscillator element circuit, were connected together via an intermediate circuit (see Figure 5.5). It contained breakout connectors for the digital & analog lines used by each FCR element, marked as CONN1 - CONN4.

The oscillator circuit element and its respective pair of FCR circuit elements were connected together via a comprehensive intermediate circuit (see Figure 5.6). It contained breakout connectors for the digital control lines connected to the FLL & Operation MCUs, marked as CONN_STABIL1 & CONN_DATA1 respectively, alongside the MCP-1700 voltage regulator that acted as the local power source for the oscillator & the FCR circuit elements, marked as REG1. This circuit element acted as the complete CO module, and contained connectors for all the digital control lines required by the frequency-locking & control logic elements.

The individual CO modules were connected to the FLL & Operation MCUs via the MCU circuit (see Figure 5.7). It contained breakout connectors for the digital control lines for all 6 CO modules, marked as CONN1 - CONN18, alongside connectors for the two Teensy 4.1 MCU components, marked as MCU_FLL1 & MCU_MAIN1. The primary power connector for the entire prototype was located in this circuit, marked as CONN_POW1, which supplied power to the entire system.

5.2  Results

The constructed PHOSCO prototype was comprehensively evaluated with the testing algorithms developed as the evaluation logic on the Operation MCU (see Section 4.3.4). This process included separate evaluations of its frequency-locking capabilities (see Section 4.3.2), its TDV storage precision & longevity (see Section 4.3.4.2) and its TDV operation precision (see Section 4.3.4.3). For each evaluation session, the evaluation data was stored on a micro-SD card connected to the Operation MCU as a series of plain-text TXT files, which was subsequently connected to a Windows system and transferred for evaluation & visualization.

The attained data pertaining to the frequency-locking capabilities showed a maximum achieved HIGH & LOW tduty duration error of ±500 CPU cycles (see Section 5.2.1). The attained storage & operation evaluation data showed ∆Φ error figures that were smaller than the pre-established overall system tolerance of ±10% of tduty (see Section 4.3.4.4). The storage precision & longevity displayed a maximum error deviation of ±9% within the tracking period of 10 seconds (see Section 5.2.2). The operation result precision data displayed a maximum error deviation of ±5% for the Copy & Inversion operations, and ±7% for the Addition & Subtraction operations, immediately after execution (see Section 5.2.3). Thus, the results pertaining to the storage & operation precision of the constructed prototype were determined to be a successful validation of the proposed PHOSCO framework (see Section 6.1).

The overall DC power draw from all implemented CO modules was measured at 40 mA, based on the reported figure on the DC power supply module. Thus, each of the 6 implemented CO modules maintained an estimated current draw of 6.7 mA, which at +5V corresponded to an estimated power draw of 33.3 mW per CO module.

5.2.1  Frequency-Locking Precision

The frequency & tduty matching capabilities of the constructed PHOSCO prototype were evaluated by executing the testing algorithm developed as part of the frequency-locking evaluation logic module (see Section 4.3.4.1), immediately after the start of a frequency-locking processes with a target tduty of 2,000 μs. The testing algorithm (see Algorithm 4.8) tracked the tduty durations of all CO modules in the prototypes system for 2,000 iterations, which corresponded to a tracking duration in excess of 100 seconds, in the absence of any TDV storage or operation processes. The resultant data was subsequently visualized with a custom Python-based visualization script (see Listing M.1).

The visualized frequency & tduty locking evaluation data (see Figure 5.26) displayed a high degree of tduty matching precision, following an initial convergence period of approximately 25 seconds. The measured tduty durations of all CO modules was observed to remain within a tolerance of ±500 CPU cycles of the target 1,200,000 CPU cycles (see Figure 5.26). This corresponded to an estimated tduty duration error of ±0.83 μs of the target tduty duration of 2,000 μs.

The noise within the observed tolerance of ±500 CPU cycles (see Figure 5.26) resembled a random scattering of measured tduty durations and was dense in nature. This noise was the likely result of random timing jitter, caused by inconsistent timing measurements of the Teensy 4.1's internal timing mechanics at the sub- μs scale. Due to the persistent & active nature of the custom frequency-locking algorithm that was implemented (see Algorithm 4.1), and a lack of an in-built duration measurement tolerance, a non-negligible amount of tduty over-correction likely also contributed to the observed noise.

5.2.2  Storage Precision

The storage precision & longevity of the TDVs simulated in the constructed PHOSCO prototype were evaluated by executing the testing algorithm developed as part of the TDV storage evaluation logic module (see Section 4.3.4.2) during an active frequency-locking process. This included the execution of the TDVSet algorithm (see Algorithm 4.2) repeatedly, over a pre-determined set of testing input values (see Figure B.1a), followed by a ∆Φ tracking session of all TDVs. Each ∆Φ tracking session was executed for 2,000 iterations, which corresponded to a tracking duration in excess of 10 seconds. The resultant ∆Φ data was subsequently visualized with a custom Python-based visualization script (see Listing M.2, lines 92–222) in several sets of graphical plots. These visualizations displayed the results corresponding to individual tracking sessions performed with each tested input (see Appendix C), alongside a set of plots that displayed the collective average & standard deviation of the measured error (see Figure 5.27). The measured error was defined as the percentage of the ∆Φ values relative to the operating tduty durations of the CO modules in the prototype.

The visualization pertaining to the collective average error and its deviation (see Figure 5.27) displayed a gradual error divergence with respect to time. The initial measured ∆Φ of all TDVs displayed a very low degree of error average & deviation within the first 0.1 seconds of measurement. While the observed average error of all TDVs remained within a range of ±5% for the entirety of the measured 10 second period, the error deviation reached an approximate magnitude of ±9% for one of the evaluated TDVs. The visualizations pertaining to the individual test input values (see Appendix C) display a more intense degree of observable noise, with some individual test runs slightly exceeding the established ±10% precision tolerance threshold.

The observed noise within the ±10% tolerance was characterized as transient in nature. The visualizations pertaining to the individual test input values displayed a set of similar noise patterns that appeared to be synchronized. In many individual visualizations, one or more of the measured TDVs' ∆Φ values would trend upwards for a brief period of time, followed by a downward trend for another brief time period. It is hypothesized that these transient trends are a manifestation of a given oscillator's relative oscillation phase being effectively pulled towards that of another oscillator.

These observations indicated a likely presence of this unintended frequency-locking over-correction, caused by the periodic pausing actions.

Conclusions

6.1  Results Overview

The gathered, processed and visualized storage & operation precision evaluation data, alongside their qualitative analysis, provided significant & valuable insight into the viability of the proposed Phase-based Oscillatory Computational (PHOSCO) framework.

The fact that all TDV operations displayed a high degree of result accuracy, immediately following execution, was deduced as a very promising sign in terms of their mathematical viability. This implied that the operational workflow of the proposed TDV operations maintains a degree of merit in terms of a computational model, and is likely to produce more accurate results in subsequent PHOSCO prototypes. Thus, these results display an exciting degree of promise in the context of the proposed PHOSCO framework.

The noise observed within all TDV storage & operation evaluation datasets was attributed to the electrical noises introduced by the inter-oscillatory coupling interactions (see Section 4.4.1). This was deduced from the nature of the observed noise interactions, with the notable presence of repeating, synchronized and directed drifting patterns, instead of random scattering disturbances. To this end, it can be concluded that the overall operational precision of PHOSCO-based computing systems is highly sensitive to inter-oscillatory coupling interactions, and likely require an increased degree of electrical isolation of it's various circuit components, compared to contemporary digital systems. From the observed results, it was deduced that the value drift of stored numerical values is directly proportional to the duration of storage. This implies that the optimal usage of a PHOSCO-based system involves rapid value storage, computation & value extraction, while minimizing the overall time period in which numerical values spend stored within the hypothetical system. To address these storage longevity concerns, and to improve the operational accuracy of potential subsequent PHOSCO implementations, several important considerations are recommended for future investigation (see Section 6.2). It is hoped that these suggestions could act as a springboard for hypothetical evolutions of the PHOSCO framework, or become an inspiration for future research efforts into the realm of unconventional computational models.

6.2  Future Works

The generalized nature of the proposed PHOSCO framework (see Section 3.1), alongside the relatively limited construction capacity encountered during the implementation of the constructed prototype (see Chapter 5), leave significant room for future improvement. There are several potential avenues for further research and development of the PHOSCO framework & future prototypes that are likely to yield improved computational results. Electrical improvements that include potential implementations of dedicated control logic circuitry (see Section 6.2.1) and a higher degree of electrical isolation (see Section 6.2.2) are likely to drastically increase the operation precision of future PHOSCO prototypes. Thorough statistical analysis & characterization of the noise patterns experienced by various noise sources could result in the creation of predictive models for noise filtering, which could drastically decrease the observed error in filtered operating results (see Section 6.2.3). A detailed analysis of power consumption requirements pertaining to the proposed PHOSCO framework, based on contemporary analog oscillator components, is likewise expected to offer an insight into the overall power demand profile of PHOSCO-based systems and a comparison to their conventional digital & analog counterparts (see Section 6.2.4). Additionally, the potential addition of non-linear arithmetic operations, such as multiplication & division, into the PHOSCO model are likely to significantly expand its limited set of potential applications (see Section 6.2.5).

6.2.1  Dedicated Control Logic

The usage of specialized control logic elements for the frequency-locking mechanics and TDV storage & operation management could result in the realization of a fully-fledged PHOSCO-based computing system.

Dedicated frequency-locking logic can be implemented by the usage of a singular reference oscillator, calibrated to operate around the desired target frequency & tduty. This reference oscillator element could then be used in real-time comparison operations implemented solely with digital circuitry, and compared directly to the oscillator modules in a hypothetical PHOSCO-based system via latching-based mechanisms. The differing outputs of the latching elements could subsequently be used to trigger and direct changes in the oscillators' tduty controller elements. This could remove the need for a digital MCU controller system for frequency-locking. Furthermore, the unintended over-corrections that were observed in the operational results of the constructed PHOSCO prototype (see Section 5.2.3), could likely be mitigated by the inclusion of supplemental corrective logic that subtracted or discarded any tduty measurements artificially affected by the oscillator pausing actions. Alternatively, dedicated high-precision Frequency-Locked Loop circuits could be used to build the CO modules of subsequent PHOSCO prototypes (see Section 4.5), provided they are combined with supplemental oscillatory pausing mechanics.

Dedicated TDV storage logic can be implemented in various ways depending on the input format of the inserted numerical values. Conventional digital-to-time & time-to-digital converter modules can be used to convert an inserted digital number to a proportional time duration and vice-versa [39]. Such workflows could subsequently be used to insert numerical values into a PHOSCO-based system and read values stored as TDVs by converting the measured ∆Φ value to a proportional digital number. Analog values stored as capacitor charges could be used to create delay-line elements, which could be used to initialize the REF & VAL oscillator elements of a hypothetical TDV module with a proportional time difference. Similarly, the time duration corresponding of a given TDV's ∆Φ could be used to charge a holding capacitor to a charge state proportional to the TDV's ∆Φ [40].

Dedicated TDV operation logic can be realized as independent modules containing their respective operation circuits, which could execute TDV operations when connected to a set of hypothetical TDVs. These operation modules can hold additional latching or delay-based elements to establish a constant tocd value for each operation type, which would likely ensure a high degree of operational accuracy in the produced results.

6.2.2  Electrical Isolation

The majority of the observed deviation & drift in terms of the error magnitude present in the results obtained during the evaluation of the constructed PHOSCO prototype were attributed to electrical noises & disturbances pertaining to concurrently-running oscillators. The most significant source of electrical noise that was encountered was caused by the electrical coupling experienced by the individual oscillator elements in each of the implemented CO modules (see Section 4.4.1). In order to reduce the overall disturbance magnitude produced by the inter-oscillator coupling iterations, each CO module could be individually isolated with the usage of optocoupler elements on each digital data line that connects them to the control & frequency-locking logic [90][91][92] (see Figure 6.1).

This approach would likely result in a significant decrease of electrical noise transmission through the individual digital lines connected to each CO module, and would allow them to be powered by separate power sources, while retaining their respective connections to the control logic. While a minimal degree of experienced signal delay for each optocoupler is expected to be introduced, the overall precision gain in subsequent prototypes due to the decrease in coupling-based noise would likely exceed any negative effects caused by the introduction of digital isolating components.

6.2.3  Noise Characterization

The precision drift observed in the longevity of stored TDV values within the constructed PHOSCO prototype (see Section 5.2.2) is primarily caused due to the oscillation drift experienced by each CO module, and due to the inter-oscillator coupling interactions. These noise sources could be effectively characterized in terms of a predictive model, as a function of time [93][94]. Such a hypothetical predictive model could subsequently be used to predict noise patterns in terms of a given TDV's ∆Φ, as a function of time, and be used to construct filtering modules. These filtering modules could be constructed purely as logical modules, or as filtering circuit elements attached to each CO module, which would likely result in a decrease of the overall observed noise levels in any filtered datasets.

Furthermore, similar characterization methodologies could be used to effectively characterize the ideal tocd values for each TDV operation type in subsequent PHOSCO prototypes. Instead of performing extensive and costly parameter-space searches for all TDV operations in each hypothetical PHOSCO-based system, the ideal tocd values for each generalized TDV operation proposed in the theoretical PHOSCO framework could likely be modeled with training data from a few evaluated prototype systems. Since the tocd parameter-space search performed in the evaluation of the constructed PHOSCO prototype could be effectively interpreted as a machine learning problem, with the set of testing inputs provided to the tocd parameter-space search forming the training dataset, dedicated deep-learning algorithms could be used to characterize ideal tocd values for each generalized TDV operation type, which could be used in subsequent PHOSCO prototypes.

6.2.4  Power Consumption Analysis

One of the primary motivations behind contemporary research of temporal computing models is tied to the considerable power efficiency advantages they maintain over their conventional digital and analog counterparts (see Section 2.2). Since the evaluation of the constructed prototype primarily focused on the validation of the theoretical PHOSCO frameworks, its observed power demand profile was not expected to demonstrate significant efficiency advantages that are characteristic of most contemporary temporal frameworks. A comprehensive analysis of the PHOSCO framework in terms of theoretical power requirements is an important consideration for hypothetical future prototypes. Such an analysis should include an evaluation of the expected power requirements with respect to the amount of oscillator modules used in a PHOSCO-based system, and is likely to provide significant insight on the relationship between the scale of a such systems and their respective power demand profile. Notably, this should be investigated in conjunction with an exploration of other contemporary analog oscillator modules [95][96][97], that operate on alternative oscillatory mechanics or with different analog oscillation mediums, to comprehensively investigate potential alternatives to the capacitor-based oscillators used in the constructed PHOSCO prototype.

6.2.5  Non-linear TDV Operations

The arithmetic TDV operations proposed in the theoretical PHOSCO framework rely on the alignment of the Operand TDV signals in order to create some linear combination corresponding to the resultant value. This approach has been demonstrated to work well for linear arithmetic operations in the constructed prototype, such as addition & subtraction. However, attempting to implement non-linear arithmetic operations with this approach, such as multiplication & division, is considerably more difficult. Some non-linear arithmetic operations, such as multiplication, can likely be implemented in a linear fashion for integer-only multiplication. However, multiplication by non-integer values, such as those stored in another TDV, would likely require additional non-digital circuit elements to perform exactly or approximate. Since a conclusive mathematical proof that disproves the possibility of the realization of multiplication & division solely with linear combinations has not yet been widely accepted, the existing alignment approach could likely be modified to perform a relatively accurate approximation algorithm for many non-linear operations.

Despite this, a slightly different approach to TDV computation, in the context of a hypothetical PHOSCO-based system, could be used for a potential implementation of some non-linear arithmetic operations, such as scalar multiplication & division. The introduction of frequency-alteration elements in the oscillator modules of a hypothetical PHOSCO-based system would theoretically allow for non-linear ∆Φ shifts between a given TDV's oscillator elements. This could be implemented similarly to the oscillator pausing functionality used in the proposed PHOSCO framework, where an oscillator's operating HIGH & LOW tduty durations could be instantaneously increased or decreased by a constant factor for an arbitrary duration of time.

Using this approach, a hypothetical TDV multiplication operation, for positive numerical values greater than 1, could be implemented as a frequency-alteration action that halves the operating frequency of one Operand TDV's VAL oscillator element, for the duration of the other Operand TDV's ∆Φ (see Figure 6.2). This would result in the altered TDV's ∆Φ being augmented by an amount corresponding to the static TDV's ∆Φ multiplied by a constant factor of 2. Similarly, a hypothetical TDV division operation, for positive numerical values greater than 1, could be implemented as a frequency-alteration action that doubles the operating frequency of one Operand TDV's VAL oscillator element, for the duration of the other Operand TDV's ∆Φ. This would result in the altered TDV's ∆Φ being decreased by an amount corresponding to the static TDV's ∆Φ divided by a constant factor of 2.

In this hypothetical approach, the frequency-alteration is used for all TDV multiplication & division operations in order to effectively scale a given TDV's ∆Φ value, dependent on the ∆Φ value of another TDV. The constant frequency-alteration factor, used by all non-linear TDV operations, would determine the effective multiplication & division range of such operations. In the context of a potential future PHOSCO prototype, this could be achieved by introducing supplemental control logic into each CO module, which could effectively redirect the charging & discharging processes of the timing capacitor through a secondary set of FCR modules, as determined by a digital control line. These secondary FCR modules would be pre-calibrated to correspond to the constant-factor offsets of the primary FCR modules, possibly controlled by the same frequency-locking logic. While this approach could theoretically provide results for arithmetic operations of multiplication & division for positive numerical values greater than 1, the viability of a mathematically pure form of such non-linear operations, which can account for all real values, remains to be investigated in the context of the theoretical PHOSCO framework.
